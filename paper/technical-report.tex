\title{\vspace{-1.8em}\textbf{A Graph-Based Control Interface for Traffic Signals\\on Heterogeneous Road Networks}}
\author{Bertil Braun}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\vspace{-2.2em}

\begin{abstract}
Traffic-signal phases differ with junction geometry, lane connectivity, and signal design, which makes a fixed neural action head difficult to reuse across road networks.
This report describes a movement-centric interface that separates representation, learned prioritization, and local action construction.
Directed \LaneGroup{} nodes hold corridor state and \Movement{} nodes represent legal controlled turns.
A shared typed message-passing network produces one scalar score per \Movement{}.
At each junction, a fixed incidence matrix aggregates those scores into logits for phases constructed automatically from SUMO controlled links and conflict data.
The learned parameter shapes therefore do not depend on network size or local phase count.
PPO experiments support the implementation: policies transfer across synthetic grid sizes and aspect ratios, execute on five heterogeneous city scenarios, and expose a clear limitation when signal coverage changes outside the training distribution.
The result is a practical representation and control pipeline, not a claim of a new reinforcement-learning algorithm or universal traffic-control generalization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

A traffic signal selects a local combination of compatible traffic movements.
The number and meaning of those combinations vary: a three-arm junction, a regular four-arm junction, and an intersection with dedicated turn lanes need not share an action schema.
Consequently, phase index~2 at one controller has no intrinsic relationship to phase index~2 at another.
A policy whose output weights correspond directly to fixed phase indices embeds assumptions about one predefined junction layout.

This implementation instead uses a \Movement{}---a legal controlled turn from an incoming corridor to an outgoing corridor---as the interface between shared learning and local signal operation.
Movements retain common semantics when their number and compatible combinations change.
The learned model can therefore emit a variable number of \Movement{} scores, after which each junction maps its own scores to its own phase set.

The report contributes an integrated implementation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a city-level graph of directed \LaneGroup{} and \Movement{} nodes;
  \item typed communication for upstream demand, downstream supply, and unsignalized pass-through context;
  \item a shared two-block message-passing policy with a scalar \Movement{} head;
  \item automatic construction of junction-specific, conflict-derived phase sets; and
  \item an OSM-to-SUMO, PPO-training, and evaluation workflow.
\end{enumerate}

Movement pressure and movement-structured control have substantial prior lineage.
Max-pressure methods score compatible stages from constituent traffic flows \cite{varaiya2013maxpressure}; PressLight connects pressure to learned control \cite{wei2019presslight}; and FRAP structures a policy around movements and phase competition \cite{zheng2019frap}.
TransferLight also maps movement representations to variable phase spaces, using a learned hierarchical phase encoder \cite{schmidt2024transferlight}.
The present report does not claim to originate movement scoring.
Its focus is the transparent combination of a city-level typed graph, a scalar learned interface, and phases generated by the network-construction pipeline.

\section{Representation and control interface}

\subsection{From road corridors to a typed graph}

A \LaneGroup{} is a directed corridor between controller-relevant endpoints.
Opposite travel directions are distinct nodes because they have different queues, speeds, capacity, and destinations.
Unambiguous continuations through unsignalized junctions may be contracted; contraction stops before a branch would create false reachability.
Dynamic features summarize the detector region near the downstream end, including vehicle and queue counts, occupancy, speed, storage, arrival and departure rates, and queue-tail arrival estimates.
Static features describe corridor scale.

A \Movement{} joins one input \LaneGroup{} to one output \LaneGroup{} through a controlled junction.
It holds turn-local features such as demand, turn class, controlled-link count, and recent green state.
Junctions own Movements and phases but are not graph nodes.

Every \Movement{} has four directed relation types:
\[
L_{\mathrm{in}}\!\rightarrow M,\quad
L_{\mathrm{out}}\!\rightarrow M,\quad
M\!\rightarrow L_{\mathrm{in}},\quad
M\!\rightarrow L_{\mathrm{out}}.
\]
Message direction is information flow rather than vehicle flow.
In particular, \(L_{\mathrm{out}}\!\rightarrow M\) returns downstream storage and spillback context to a turn that would feed that corridor.
At an unsignalized junction, legal pass-through connections become weighted \LaneGroup-to-\LaneGroup{} edges instead of controllable \Movement{} nodes.
Their weight decays with connector free-flow time \(t_{\mathrm{ff}}\):
\[
w=\exp\!\left(-t_{\mathrm{ff}}/30\,\mathrm{s}\right).
\]

\subsection{Two update blocks, not graph attention}

The active model is a typed mean-aggregation graph neural network, not a graph attention network.
LaneGroups and Movements are embedded separately.
For update block \(k\), the essential computation is
\begin{align}
M^{k+1} &=
 \phi_M\!\left(M^k,
 \operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{in}}(L^k),
 \operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{out}}(L^k)\right),\\
L^{k+1} &=
 \phi_L\!\left(L^k,
 \operatorname{mean}_{M\rightarrow\mathrm{in}}(M^{k+1})
 + \operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{conn}}(L^k),
 \operatorname{mean}_{M\rightarrow\mathrm{out}}(M^{k+1})\right).
\end{align}
Each relation has its own linear transformation; neighbours are averaged rather than assigned learned attention weights.

Two blocks are used.
The distinction is important: initialization and the first block give a Movement its endpoint corridor state, while the second block is the first opportunity for one Movement to affect another through a shared \LaneGroup{} (\autoref{fig:representation}).
The depth is configurable, but this report neither assumes that more blocks are always beneficial nor presents an optimal-depth claim.
The final Movement embeddings are mapped to scalar scores \(s_m\).
The critic mean-pools the Movements owned by a junction and estimates one value per controller.

\subsection{Automatic phases and local action mapping}

OSM tags do not directly define the action space used by the policy.
The build pipeline uses SUMO \texttt{netconvert} \cite{alvarezlopez2018sumo} to obtain lane connections, controlled-link indices, request indices, and request-conflict (\texttt{foes}) data.
Links that must activate together are first combined into atomic groups.
An atomic group is rejected if it contains an internal conflict.
Two groups are incompatible when SUMO reports a conflict in either direction or when different incoming approaches merge into the same outgoing edge.

The compatible groups form a graph.
Bron--Kerbosch enumeration \cite{bron1973cliques} returns every maximal clique, and each maximal compatible set becomes one selectable green phase.
``Maximal'' matters: a smaller protected-turn set remains an action when no additional compatible group can be added, even if another phase contains more Movements.
This is conflict-derived construction under the implemented SUMO and merge rules, not formal verification of a real signal plan.

For junction \(j\), let \(A_j\in\{0,1\}^{|P_j|\times|M_j|}\) record which local Movements are green in each selectable phase.
The shared GNN produces \(\mathbf{s}_j\), and phase logits are simply
\[
\boldsymbol{\ell}_j=A_j\mathbf{s}_j,
\qquad
\ell_{j,p}=\sum_m A_{j,p,m}s_m.
\]
An availability mask removes phases that cannot be chosen at the current decision.
The remaining logits define a local categorical policy.
This fixed sum is an explicit, inspectable interface and inductive bias; it is not claimed to be the only or optimal phase aggregation.

\section{Training and evaluation}

PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo} is used as an optimizer and is not re-derived here.
The anchored controller makes a decision every \(5\,\mathrm{s}\), starts an immediate \(3\,\mathrm{s}\) yellow when switching, and requires one decision of minimum green.
Training uses two message-passing blocks, four PPO epochs, 200 decisions per rollout, and entropy coefficient \(0.001\).
Its local reward combines vehicle progress, discharge, braking, and gridlock terms with weights \(1/10/10/0.02\); global, throughput, flow, and direct switching terms are zero.

Mixed graph sizes require one practical correction: equal rollout counts are not equal PPO weight because larger graphs yield more junction actions per transition.
Rollouts are therefore allocated to approximately balance the number of local action samples contributed by each training shape or city.

Three evaluations answer progressively less controlled questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Grid size and shape.} Three independently trained mixed-grid policies saw square and rectangular grids with longest axis at most five. They were evaluated without further learning on ten geometries, including \(6\times6\), using six fresh traffic seeds.
  \item \textbf{Signal coverage.} Removing controllers tests whether dense-signal training transfers to graphs where unsignalized pass-through edges replace controlled Movements. A separate \(50\%\)-coverage training run tests whether exposure to those gaps changes the outcome.
  \item \textbf{Cities.} One policy was trained with rollouts from Karlsruhe, Mannheim, Heidelberg, and Freiburg and evaluated on those scenarios plus Stuttgart. All runs use the fixed terminal checkpoint at iteration~60; training duration was not selected by stopping on an evaluation peak.
\end{itemize}

Sampled execution is primary because it matches the policy optimized by PPO.
Greedy execution and full per-seed results are retained in the appendix.
Max pressure is the main traffic-control baseline, with queue, fixed-time, and uniform-random policies as additional references.

\section{Results}

Left: the sampled mixed-grid policy transfers to the larger full-coverage \(6\times6\) grid across all evaluated demands (three training seeds and six fresh traffic seeds). Right: city throughput and completion relative to the strongest baseline for each metric, showing both successful and mixed cases.}
  \label{fig:results}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\textbf{Synthetic topology transfer.}
At full signal coverage, the learned policy retained useful behaviour as grid size and aspect ratio changed.
On \(6\times6\), sampled throughput rose from \(4344\) to \(5174\) vehicles/hour across demand scales \(0.6\) to \(0.8\), compared with \(4168\) to \(4521\) for max pressure.
Completion improved by \(3.5\), \(6.6\), and \(10.6\) percentage points at the three demands.
Transposed \(5\times2/2\times5\) and \(5\times3/3\times5\) pairs were also similar.
These results support reuse across sizes and shapes generated by the same synthetic process; they do not establish transfer to arbitrary topology.

\textbf{Coverage is distribution-sensitive.}
A policy trained only on dense signals deteriorated sharply when half or more eligible controllers were removed.
Training on several \(50\%\)-coverage layouts recovered throughput and completion near max pressure on fresh \(25\%\) and \(50\%\) layouts, but did not yield uniformly good waiting density or higher-coverage behaviour.
The graph can carry information through unsignalized gaps, but the policy must encounter the changed control distribution during training.

\textbf{City behaviour is heterogeneous.}
The city graphs range from 286 to 708 LaneGroups, 271 to 508 Movements, and 41 to 84 policy-controlled junctions.
One parameter set runs on all five.
Sampled control is clearly positive in Karlsruhe and improves Stuttgart throughput/completion over every baseline.
It is close to fixed time in Heidelberg, trails queue control in Mannheim, and improves Freiburg throughput and completion while not improving wait density.
The five-city mean is \(3345.6\) vehicles/hour and \(64.93\%\) completion, versus \(3036.8\) and \(59.37\%\) for max pressure.
The city experiment is one training run and should be read as a feasibility study, not a general city-transfer estimate.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

\begin{table}[H]
  \small
  \centering
  \caption{Intended interpretation of the evaluation evidence.}
  \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
    \toprule
    Evaluation & Supports & Does not establish\\
    \midrule
    Synthetic grids & Size/shape reuse within the grid generator & Transfer to arbitrary topology\\
    Signal coverage & Diagnosis of a control-distribution shift & Coverage-invariant control\\
    Five cities & Execution feasibility and heterogeneous outcomes & A general city-transfer estimate\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
  \label{tab:evidence-scope}
\end{table}

The architecture removes fixed graph and phase dimensions, but structural compatibility is not distributional robustness.
Sparse signal coverage, different demand, and irregular city traffic can still change what the same local observations mean.
Detector features are local, the phase sum assumes additive Movement utilities, and the conflict rules inherit the fidelity of the imported SUMO network.
Sampled and greedy execution also differ materially, so deterministic argmax control cannot be substituted silently for the trained policy.

Within those boundaries, the implementation provides a compact way to connect a shared graph policy to heterogeneous traffic networks.
LaneGroups give traffic state a directed corridor representation; Movements provide reusable controlled primitives; the second message-passing block introduces neighbouring Movement context; and deterministic phase construction keeps local compatibility outside the learned model.
The synthetic experiments give the clearest evidence for size and shape reuse, while coverage and city results identify where training distribution remains decisive.

\paragraph{Sampled versus greedy.}
Greedy city control averaged \(3182.6\) vehicles/hour, \(61.89\%\) completion, and \(0.980\,\mathrm{s/m}\) wait density, compared with \(3345.6\), \(64.93\%\), and \(0.613\,\mathrm{s/m}\) for sampled control.
The gap motivates using the sampled policy as the primary result.

\paragraph{Coverage study.}
Dense-signal training transferred poorly to reduced coverage.
A separate policy trained on five \(50\%\)-coverage \(4\times4\) layouts matched max-pressure throughput and completion closely on fresh \(25\%\) and \(50\%\) layouts under sampled execution.
At \(75\%\), sampled throughput and completion were lower; greedy control retained throughput but accumulated more wait.
This one-seed study diagnoses a training-distribution effect rather than establishing coverage-invariant control.

\paragraph{Evaluation units.}
Grid means should distinguish three independent policy-training seeds from the six traffic seeds used to evaluate each policy.
The city study contains one policy-training seed and three traffic seeds.
No claim in the main text relies on treating all traffic episodes as independent policy replications.
